From the innkeeperFerry Lane, Polwenna · 1 December 2026

“We have kept a lamp in the window for two hundred and forty winters, and we are not about to stop.”

Dear reader,

My great-great-grandmother ran this house with a rowing boat and a bell. When the bell rang at the far slip, somebody put down their pint and rowed. We have an engine now, and a timetable, but the arrangement is the same: if you are on the other side, we will come and fetch you.

This winter we have redone four rooms under the eaves, let the kitchen cook less and cook it better, and moved the oyster weekend to February, when the water is coldest and the oysters are at their best.

Come for one night or for a week. Bring boots. The fire in the snug is lit by four.

Maud PenhallowInnkeeper, the fifth Penhallow to hold the licence

Written from
the snug, table by the fire

P.S. Dogs are welcome in the bar and in rooms 1, 2 and 9.

04The storyNo. 31 · Winter 2026–27

The last boat home

How a rowing boat, a bell and one stubborn family kept a river crossing open since the reign of George III.

Words by Tamsin RoweDrawings by Jory Kemp9 minutes to read

The crossing at Polwenna is older than the inn. Drovers walked cattle down to the water here long before anyone thought to sell them beer, and the first ferry was simply whoever owned a boat and could be woken. The licence came in 1782, granted to one Jacob Penhallow on condition that he keep “a sound boat and a light”.11The licence hangs behind the bar. The light it mentions was a tallow lamp; the window it stood in is now room four.

He kept both. The house grew around the slip a room at a time: a taproom first, then a kitchen, then bedrooms for the travellers who missed the tide and had nowhere else to go. By 1850 the inn had twelve rooms and the boat had a name.

“If the bell rang, you went. It didn’t matter who it was or what the weather was doing.”

Bill Penhallow, ferryman 1961–1998

The engine came in 1924 and the timetable in 1951, but the rule about the bell never changed. It still hangs on the far slip, and it is still answered, even after the last scheduled boat.22Ring before 23:30 and the crossing is free. After that, it is still free, but expect a look.

Five Penhallows, one licence

  • Jacob, 1782–1819: built the taproom
  • Grace, 1819–1866: added the rooms
  • Thomas, 1866–1921: named the boat
  • Bill, 1961–1998: the last to row
  • Maud, since 2009: the kitchen as it is now

Today the Jenny Wren makes the crossing forty times a day in summer and twenty-six in winter. The bell is bronze, slightly cracked, and louder than it looks.

08The kitchenNo. 31 · Winter 2026–27

What the river brings

The menu is written at eleven, after the boats come in, and it is short on purpose: three things from the river, two from the land, and a pudding Maud’s grandmother would recognise.

Lunch is served in the bar and on the quay. Supper is in the long room upstairs, by candle, with the river going past the windows in the dark.

“We cook less, and we cook it better.”Ollie Tregear, head cook

If you are on the other side of the river and the bell rings, somebody will come and fetch you.

The rule of the house, since 1782

12LettersNo. 31 · Winter 2026–27

You asked

The questions that arrive most often, with our answers.

Write to us
Can we arrive by boat of our own?From a reader in Falmouth

A.Yes. There are four visitor moorings off the quay, free for guests. Call the bar on VHF 12 when you are in the river.

Is there parking at the inn?

A.Eight spaces on the lane, first come. The station car park across the river is larger, and the ferry is free for guests.

Do you cater for vegetarians, and for allergies?From a reader in Truro

A.Always one dish from the land without meat, and the kitchen will adapt most others. Tell us when you book.
